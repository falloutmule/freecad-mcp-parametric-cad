# Character before mechanism

Blockfolk reads through an oversized square head, a simple expressive face, stepped hair, compact torso, substantial hands and boots, and simplified layered clothing. A cream shirt and purple/brown vest describe the reference character, not a mandatory palette or costume. A different character may have different proportions, clothing, hair and articulation.

Use a few integrated clothing volumes: sleeve caps and cuffs, broad trouser fronts, hems, boot uppers/soles, garment shoulders, belt and collar. Keep hands visibly distinct from forearms and boots from shins. A thumb block and stepped sole can communicate more than dozens of tiny grooves. Preserve features in monochrome because CAD colors are not single-material STL geometry. Avoid detached decorative solids.

## Early visual gate

Before detailed collision grids, mesh export or slicing, capture actual front/side/rear/isometric views, neutral standing, sitting and one arm raised. Use a consistent camera scale, unclipped extents, useful lighting and a monochrome check. Compare with the intended reference, including all major views rather than only an attractive front render.

Ask: **“Does this still read as the intended Blockfolk character, or has the joint mechanism become the visual identity?”** Review head/body balance, arm and leg substance, recognizable hands/boots, clothing layers, front/back depth and transitions. A mechanically valid robot with a large head fails. Exposed half barrels, thin skeletal straps, absent shoulder walls or oversized rear padding are reasons to redesign now.

Declare each view PASS or REVISE with a concrete observation; no automatic image score. The reference product pass concealed much of the mechanism with faceted sleeves and broad trouser faces, rolled cuffs, solid hands/thumbs, boot layers, vest/belt and stepped hair. Some medial/rear openings and shoulder seams remain. “Integrated” does not mean every moving seam is invisible.

## Integrating shells without breaking joints

Reserve the whole journal/bore, cap envelope, follower chamber, release gap and swept limb volumes before adding clothing. Cut added shells by actual moving envelopes plus process clearance, then verify complete remaining capture material. Do not trim a retaining ring merely to hide an interference. Never replace a full hoop with two apparently strong tabs without a new capture argument.

Leave garment transitions generous enough for pose changes, but do not erase the arm cross section. A sleeve can bury a shoulder; a cuff can visually bridge an elbow seam; a trouser front can cover a hip without filling its chamber. Evaluate these in sitting and raised-arm views, where neutral-only masks often fail.

Every shell edit touching a joint reopens solid connectivity, free-motion, capture, intended-contact and slicer checks. Cosmetic color-only edits can use a zero symmetric-difference comparison to show geometry was unchanged. Boolean repair is not progress if it destroys the product architecture.

## Scale decisions

Change height, head ratios and limb lengths independently of process gaps. At 60 mm, a reference-sized hinge may consume too much of a limb. Consider fewer articulated locations, another family, less joint range or revised clothing before shrinking gaps or compliant walls. At larger scales, reassess limb mass, cap strength, leaf stress and holding behavior. Neither multiplying all STL coordinates nor copying one costume is a reusable design method.
